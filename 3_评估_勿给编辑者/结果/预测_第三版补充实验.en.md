## B1: stronger generic baselines for reading the weights

### Input and scoring (exactly the same as R1/R2)
- Input: `W1` (2268×1024) of `1_阶段1_读权重/net_q7.npz`, permutation `perm = np.random.default_rng(777).permutation(2268)` (the same as `reading_baseline.py`; row i of q7 = original input perm[i], original input index = 28·t + f).
- Main metric (the same `score` as R1/R2): a group counts as "correct" = exactly 81 rows, all from the same original feature, with the order within the group strictly monotone in true time (forward or backward, adjacent differences exactly ±1). The number of correct groups out of 28 is reported.
- Secondary metrics (new, only as auxiliary description):
  - number of pure groups: groups of exactly 81 rows all from the same feature (R2 reported the same metric);
  - Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) between the grouping and the true features;
  - within pure groups, the median of |Spearman ρ| between the within-group order and true time.
- All methods use only the rows of W1 and no information about the answer (no prior beyond "28 chains, each of 81": the two numbers 28 groups and 81 per group are given as known inputs, as for R2, because the prompt of 1B also gave the two dimensions 2268 and 42, while 81×28 was inferred by 1C/1B themselves; here this is kept consistent with R2).

### Methods and parameter grid (fixed in advance, all reported)
Similarity S = row cosine similarity (the same as R1/R2, not centred).

**G1: kNN graph + spectral clustering into 28 groups + within-group Fiedler ordering**
- kNN graph: each row is connected to the k rows with the highest cosine similarity, symmetrized by union; edge weight = max(S, 0).
- Spectral clustering: `sklearn.cluster.SpectralClustering(n_clusters=28, affinity='precomputed', random_state=0)`, label assignment ∈ {`kmeans` (n_init=10), `cluster_qr`}.
- Within-group ordering: exactly the same as R2 (ordering by the Fiedler vector of the unnormalized Laplacian of the dense within-group max(S,0) submatrix).
- Grid: k ∈ {5, 10, 20, 40} × 2 assignment methods = 8 settings.

**G2: spectral ordering of the whole graph (spectral seriation, the approach of Atkins et al. 1998)**
- Take the Fiedler vector of the unnormalized Laplacian of the whole graph, arrange the 2268 rows into one sequence, and cut it in order into 28 segments of 81 rows each, as 28 chains.
- Graphs: dense max(S,0), and the kNN graphs of G1 with k ∈ {5, 10, 20, 40} (if the graph is disconnected the Fiedler vector degenerates; this is reported as it is).
- Grid: 5 settings.

**G3: the idea of Le Roux et al. (NeurIPS 2007) "Learning the 2-D Topology of Images"**
The original method (checked online): correlation coefficients ρ between pixels → distance D = sqrt(−log|ρ|) → k-nearest-neighbour graph (k = 4 in the original) → Isomap embedding into 2 dimensions → aligned to a grid.
Here each row of W1 is treated as a "pixel", with ρ = Pearson correlation between rows (cosine after centring the rows).
- **G3a (the original method as is)**: Isomap embedding into 2 dimensions (sklearn `Isomap(n_neighbors=k, n_components=2, metric='precomputed')`; if the graph is disconnected, sklearn adds edges automatically), then aligned to the 28×81 grid: sort along coordinate 1 and cut into 28 segments of 81 rows each, sorting within each segment by coordinate 2; plus the other orientation with the two coordinates swapped. k ∈ {4, 8, 16} × 2 orientations = 6 settings.
- **G3b (adapted to "multiple one-dimensional chains")**: the same distance and k-nearest-neighbour graph; compute geodesic distances on the graph (disconnected pairs are set to 2 times the largest finite geodesic distance), split into 28 groups by average-linkage hierarchical clustering, and order within each group by 1-dimensional classical MDS (i.e. 1-dimensional Isomap) on the geodesic distances. k ∈ {4, 8, 16} = 3 settings.

22 settings in total. Running times are reported as well.

### Predictions
- G1: with small k (5 or 10) it can recover most of the feature chains, **at least one setting ≥ 20/28**; clearly worse at k = 40. Reason: 1B observed that the most similar rows of each row are adjacent time steps of the same feature; the kNN graph with small k is almost 28 mutually disconnected chains, and spectral clustering is good at cutting nearly disconnected components. The uncertainty is whether the within-group Fiedler ordering will misorder the rows on the far "noise floor".
- G2: all settings ≤ 2/28. Reason: a single global sequence cannot represent 28 mutually unrelated chains at once.
- G3a: all settings ≤ 5/28 (the 2-dimensional grid assumption does not suit a layout in which "the features have no order among them").
- G3b: with small k, similar to G1 (≥ 15/28).
- Decision rules for the wording of the paper (fixed in advance):
  - If any G1 or G3b setting is ≥ 20/28: this means "generic local graph methods can do it too", and "the LLM's contribution was choosing the right method" in the abstract and the Discussion is narrowed to: the LLM chose a standard class of local nearest-neighbour graph methods, and what failed was only "global" clustering (R2); the LLM's specific contribution is downgraded to "recognizing the chain structure and giving a testable explanation", not finding a method that people would not think of. The sensitivity of the results to k is also reported truthfully.
  - If all generic methods are ≤ 5/28: strengthen the original claim, i.e. the generic methods (including the Le Roux method designed specifically to recover pixel topology) all fail, and 1B's observation of "two strong, two weak nearest neighbours" is indeed the key.
  - In between: describe truthfully, stating which settings partly succeed.

## B2: test of the noise hypothesis (H1 applied to further-trained networks)

### Settings
- Networks (all in `3_评估_勿给编辑者/网络/`, not modified):
  - `reference_samestate_lr1e-4_plus2000steps.npz` (same AdamW state, lr 1e-4, +2000 steps)
  - `reference_lr1e-4_plus2000steps.npz` (new AdamW state, lr 1e-4, +2000 steps)
  - `reference_plus2000steps.npz`, `reference_plus4000steps.npz` (same state, lr 1e-3, +2000/+4000 steps, controls)
  - In addition, the subject network `subject_net.npz` is recomputed with the same script, as a numerical consistency check (it should reproduce the reported B +0.40/+0.44, C +0.39/+0.46).
- Rule: the H1 frozen in `heur.py` (each network selects its own lowest 18 offsets by its own row norms), in two versions: with b2 compensation (`H1(w)`) and without compensation (`zero_comp(w, rownorm_mask(w)[0], comp=False)`). The 40,000 frames of A used for the compensation are the same as before (seed 0).
- Evaluation: the same as `confirm_eval.py` / `summarize_confirm.py`. Compute per-utterance counts of correct frames on B and C; difference = H1(network) − the network itself (not relative to the subject); bootstrap by utterance and by speaker, 2000 times each, rng seed 0, 95% percentile intervals.
- Row-norm profiles: the same as Fig 1 (reshape W1 into 81×28×1024, take the norm over 1024, then average over the 28 features), with the five networks overlaid in one figure. Numerical metric of the "far floor" = mean row norm at |Δt| ∈ 30…38 (the region zeroed by 2A); also report the median at |Δt| ≥ 26 (the metric used in the Fig 1 script).
- Also report the offsets selected by H1 on each network.

### The noise hypothesis has two versions, with different predictions
1. **Equilibrium version (in the style of Mandt et al.)**: the far weights fluctuate in a stationary distribution centred near 0, with a fluctuation amplitude that grows with the learning rate. After lowering the learning rate, the distribution contracts → the far floor drops → H1 has less noise to delete → **the gain of H1 becomes clearly smaller**. Further training at lr 1e-3: the floor is unchanged, the gain of H1 is unchanged. (This is the prediction written in the task description.)
2. **Accumulated random-walk version**: the far weights have no restoring force (AdamW's weight decay is 0.01×lr; 2000 steps at lr 1e-4 shrink them by only about 0.2%), the noise has accumulated over 9,400 steps, and lowering the learning rate only stops it from growing further, without removing it → the far floor is essentially unchanged, **the gain of H1 is essentially unchanged**. Further training at lr 1e-3: the floor keeps rising (random-walk amplitude ∝ √steps), the gain of H1 is unchanged or larger.
3. **Non-noise explanation** (the far inputs themselves have a negative effect, and deleting them amounts to regularization or to shortening the context window): the gain of H1 is about the same on all networks, regardless of the height of the far floor.

### Decision rules fixed in advance
Let g(N) = the gain of H1 on network N (pp), and g0 = the gain on the subject network for the same test set and the same version (with/without compensation).
- **Supports the equilibrium version**: g of both lr 1e-4 networks on B and C, in both versions, is ≤ 0.5·g0; at the same time g of both lr 1e-3 networks is ≥ 0.5·g0; and the far floor (mean over |Δt| 30…38) of the lr 1e-4 networks is at least 10% lower than that of the subject.
- **Does not support the equilibrium version**: g of both lr 1e-4 networks is > 0.5·g0 (the gain has not "become clearly smaller"). Then look at the far floor: an essentially unchanged floor (change < 10%) is compatible with both the random-walk version and the non-noise explanation; this experiment cannot distinguish them, and the paper says so.
- All other cases are recorded as "inconsistent results" and reported item by item.

### My expectation (written down for comparison afterwards; it does not affect the decision rules)
I lean towards version 2: weight decay has almost no effect within 2000 steps, and Adam's random movement per step at lr 1e-4 is only 1/10 of what it was, so the far floor probably will not drop noticeably; so I expect H1 still to give a clear gain on the lr 1e-4 networks (guess ≥ +0.25 pp), i.e. **does not support the equilibrium version**. For the networks trained further at lr 1e-3, the far floor rises slightly, and the gain of H1 is similar to or slightly larger than on the subject. Not very confident (about six in ten): if the far weights really are pulled by the loss function (they add noise to the hidden units, so the expected gradient pushes them towards 0), the equilibrium version could also hold.

## Addendum (2026-11-04 08:16 UTC, written after seeing the results of the preset B1 grid)

All 22 preset B1 settings gave 0/28 (results in `reading_baseline_generic_out.txt`); my predictions for G1 and G3b failed.
Diagnosis (only for explanation, not a new method): the 1st and 2nd nearest neighbours of each row are 99% adjacent time steps of the same feature, the 3rd and 4th about 90%, while only 4% of the 5th nearest neighbours are same-feature rows with |Δt|≤2, and 15% come from other features. So the kNN graph with k ≥ 5 is connected, and spectral clustering cannot cut it.

**Additional exploratory settings (not in the preset grid; added after seeing the results; they will be marked separately in the paper)**: G1 with k ∈ {2, 3, 4} (two assignment methods, 6 settings in total), everything else the same as G1.
Note: choosing k = 2 itself uses 1B's observation ("two strong, two weak"), so even if it succeeds, it cannot count as a "generic method that does not know the answer"; it only shows that "the key is choosing the right neighbourhood scale".
Prediction: k = 2 recovers a substantial part (≥ 10/28), clearly worse at k = 3, 4.
