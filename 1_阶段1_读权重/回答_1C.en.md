# Final answer of window 1C (original weights net_k3.npz)

> Copied and saved by the user from the conversation.

I will first take the weights apart with Python: look at the structure of the input dimensions, the correlations and spatial regularities between rows of W1, and the relations between classes in W3.

## Observations

**1. The input is an 81 × 28 grid, arranged as `i = 28·t + c`** (from the numbers)
- Treating the norm of each row of W1 as a sequence of length 2268, the autocorrelation is highest at lags 28, 56, 84 (0.99, 0.97, 0.93); the main FFT peak is at k=81, corresponding to period 28.
- Weight vectors of adjacent rows in the same column are very similar, cosine similarity 0.8–0.9. Adjacent columns in the same row have a similarity of only about ±0.1. So the dimension of size 81 is a smooth axis, and the dimension of size 28 is a set of features that are essentially decorrelated from each other.
- From the shape alone, 2268 has many decompositions (36×63, 42×54, 12×189, etc.). It is this periodicity evidence that establishes 81×28.

**2. The middle frame is the most important** (from the numbers)
- The norm map peaks at rows 40–41: 13.3 for column 27, 10.9 for column 0. It decays symmetrically on both sides, down to about 1.2–2.0 at the edges.
- Even the row with the smallest weights has a norm of about 0.93; there is no input that is completely unused.

**3. Properties of each of the 28 columns** (from the numbers)
- Columns 0 and 27 are the most important. Measured by class separability, both are 6.8, column 2 is 3.3, column 1 is 2.7, and it then decreases monotonically, down to only about 0.6 for columns 13–25.
- Smoothness over time also decreases with the column number: 0.91 for column 0, 0.57 for column 23.
- Column 26 is special: it is almost white noise over time (similarity at lag 1 is 0.13), its correlation with any other column at any lag is around 0.01, the norms of its rows are all about the same (about 1.7), and it has no central peak.
- I tested the possibility that "some columns are the differences (deltas) of other columns": the odd-symmetric part of the cross-lag correlations does not exceed 0.05, so this is ruled out.

**4. Output layer** (from the numbers)
- The bias of class 0 is b3=1.25, far higher than the other classes (the second highest is 0.53). When the input is set to its mean (x=0), the network also predicts class 0.
- The W3 columns of classes 40 and 41 have a cosine similarity of 0.81, and 78% of their weights are negative. On smooth noise inputs, their mean logit is about −17.8, while the other classes are between −1 and −6. Even activation maximization cannot push their probability above 0.04. This is exactly the trace that "classes almost never seen in the training set" would leave.

## Reasoning

**Classes: first assume they are phonemes, then test**
- Mapping classes 1–39 in alphabetical order to the 39 ARPAbet phonemes (AA AE AH … Z ZH), the most similar class pairs in W3 are almost all consistent with phonetics:
  - AA and AO, AW, AY; B and P, V, M; CH and SH, JH, T; D and T, N, Z
  - ER and R; G and K; N and NG, M, D; S and Z, SH; TH and F, DH; UH and UW; ZH and SH, JH
- **Quantitative test**: constructing similarities between phonemes from a set of phonetic features (manner, place, voicing) and correlating them with the cosine matrix of W3 gives r=0.45. The maximum over 5000 random permutations is only 0.17; the z value is 12.8.
- Shifting the whole mapping by one position drops r to about 0. Switching to the alphabetical order of TIMIT-39 gives at best only 0.25.
- **Rarity also fits**: the most suppressed real classes in W3 are, in order, ZH, OY, UH, which happen to be some of the rarest phonemes in English.
- **Class 0 is silence**: its gradient on the energy column (column 27) is −7.1, the lowest of all classes.

**Input: cepstral features + energy**
- Using smooth noise as input, compute the mean gradient template of each class (from the numbers):
  - Column 0 is very negative for fricatives: S −9.0, SH −7.5, CH −6.9, F −5.6. It is positive for nasals and back vowels: M +5.8, NG +4.5, OW +3.8.
    - This is the typical behaviour of c1 (spectral tilt).
    - It cannot be energy: if it were energy, S would not be lower than silence.
  - Column 27 is positive on vowels, and negative on silence (−7.1), on the closures of P/K/B/T/G, and on the weak fricatives F/TH. The sibilants S/SH/Z/ZH are on the high side, the weak fricatives F/TH on the low side.
    - This is the behaviour of log energy (or c0).
  - Column 2 separates front vowels and palatals (Y, IY, ZH, JH) from back vowels (AA, AO, OW), and is related to F2.
- The columns are essentially decorrelated from each other, and importance and smoothness both decrease monotonically with the column number. This fits cepstra arranged by order (features after a DCT).
- Candidates ruled out:
  - Log-mel filter bank: adjacent bands are highly correlated, which would make the weights of adjacent columns very similar; this does not match the observations.
  - Images: both axes of an image should be smooth.
  - Whole-sequence classification (e.g. EEG or action recognition): would not produce a symmetric structure peaking at the centre frame.
  - Sensor-based human activity recognition: same reason.

**Time direction and frame rate**
- The energy template of the stops P, T, K is: strongly negative near the centre frame (closure), turning positive about 4–6 frames later (release, followed by a vowel). This shows that larger row numbers are later in time.
- The full width at half maximum of the class templates is between 3–10 frames. If the frame shift is 10 ms, this corresponds to 30–100 ms, exactly the order of magnitude of phoneme durations. But this point is only "consistent", and does not count as strong evidence.

## Answer

- **Input data**: frame-level acoustic features of English speech (MFCC-like).
- **Input structure**:
  - 81 frames × 28 dimensions per frame, arranged time-major: `x[28·t + c]`, where t=0…80.
  - Frame 40 is the frame to be classified, with 40 frames of context on each side. At a 10 ms frame shift, this is about ±0.4 s.
  - The 28 dimensions of each frame:
    - Columns 0–25: cepstral coefficients in increasing order, most likely c1…c26. The higher-order columns (about columns 13–25) carry little information.
    - Column 26: an auxiliary channel that is approximately white noise and contains almost no information; what exactly it is cannot be determined.
    - Column 27: log energy or c0.
- **Task**: frame-level phoneme classification. That is, the acoustic model in hybrid DNN-HMM speech recognition, or a frame-level phoneme recognizer trained on forced-alignment labels. The loss is frame-level cross-entropy.
- **The 42 classes**:
  - 0 = silence (SIL)
  - 1–39 = the 39 CMU/ARPAbet phonemes with stress markers removed, in alphabetical order:
    - 1–10: AA AE AH AO AW AY B CH D DH
    - 11–20: EH ER EY F G HH IH IY JH K
    - 21–30: L M N NG OW OY P R S SH
    - 31–39: T TH UH UW V W Y Z ZH
  - 40, 41 = two non-phoneme labels that almost never occur in the training data. They may be "unknown or noise" labels such as SPN/NSN (spoken noise, non-speech noise), or they may be padding labels.
  - The training corpus may be an English corpus that uses the CMU dictionary, such as LibriSpeech or WSJ; this point is a guess.

## What evidence would make me change my conclusions

- **Class mapping**: if, given the real inputs, class 0 turned out to correspond to high-energy frames, or the similarity structure of W3 could be fitted clearly better by another label order (for example a non-English phoneme set), I would overturn this mapping.
- **Feature interpretation**: if the features from column 13 onward were shown to be another kind of feature (for example pitch, zero-crossing rate) rather than higher-order cepstra, I would revise the interpretation "28 dimensions = cepstra + energy". There is an anomalous correlation of 0.55 between columns 13 and 14, which is one point I have not yet been able to explain.
